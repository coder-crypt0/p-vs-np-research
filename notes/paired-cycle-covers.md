# SAT as a paired cycle-cover problem

The determinant reduction uses cancellation inside an OR clause when all its literals are false. For a decision problem we can change the clause so that a false clause has no local cycle cover. This leaves one condition on the global cycle cover. Each pair of reciprocal crossing edges must be used together or left unused.

## An OR block based on matching capacity

For a clause with at most three literals, use one vertex per literal port. Add two auxiliary vertices $A,B$ for a ternary clause, both with loops. The first port has reciprocal edges to $A$, the middle port to both $A$ and $B$, and the last port to $B$. These are all the internal edges. If all ports survive, their three rows have only two eligible columns, so no cycle cover exists. Every set of at most two surviving ports can be matched to distinct auxiliaries. Use the two-cycles on those pairs and the loops on any unused auxiliaries. A binary clause has one auxiliary joined reciprocally to both ports and a loop on the auxiliary. A unit clause is a port with no internal edge.

Connect these blocks to the directed-cycle equality blocks and the negation relays from the determinant reduction. The equality block has a cycle cover exactly when all its ports survive or all are deleted. In the negation relay, the auxiliary has no loop, and each port can only make a two-cycle with the auxiliary. Its cycle cover exists exactly when one port survives. Each wire contributes two reciprocal directed edges. Using both covers its two endpoints and deletes their rows and columns from the local blocks. The remaining cycle covers exist exactly when the chosen wires encode a satisfying assignment.

Splitting each vertex into a row copy and column copy turns a cycle cover into a bipartite perfect matching. The graph has maximum degree three, and different wire pairs have disjoint endpoints. This gives a direct SAT reduction to the unweighted feasibility case of parity matching, with the additional restriction that the two edges of each pair are reciprocal in the directed representation. ## Fractional balance accepts unsatisfiable formulas

Consider the doubly stochastic edge variables for ordinary bipartite perfect matching with an added equality for each wire's two directions. The projection to the wire weights is exactly the elementary clause relaxation. In the equality block, the successive cycle edges force all wire weights of a variable to agree. In a negation block, the edge weights incident to its auxiliary force the two wire weights to sum to one. In an OR block with $k$ ports, each port $i$ must send local mass $1-x_i$, where $x_i$ is the weight of its wire. The $k-1$ auxiliary columns have total capacity $k-1$, so $\sum_i x_i\ge1$.

Conversely, a Boolean-variable relaxation with every clause sum at least one extends to feasible edge variables. For a ternary clause, send the first and last ports' local masses to their respective auxiliaries. Distribute the middle port's mass across the remaining capacity. Send the same masses on the reverse edges and fill each auxiliary's unused capacity with its loop. The binary and unit capacities give the same conclusion directly. Equality and negation blocks extend by setting each local edge to the mass left over at its port.

In particular, every formula of ternary clauses has a fractional solution with wire weights $1/2$, regardless of satisfiability. There is a solution assigning positive weight to every allowed edge. At a ternary clause, give the two edges from the middle port weight $1/4$ each, the outer-port edges weight $1/2$, their reverse edges the same weights, and both auxiliary loops $1/4$. All other allowed edges have weight $1/2$. Multiplying by four gives a regular bipartite multigraph that decomposes into four perfect matchings. Their mean is balanced, even on the unsatisfiable input containing all eight signed clauses on three variables.

More generally, the weight of every edge in this mean is strictly positive. Any cycle cover can occur with positive weight in a convex decomposition of the mean. Subtract one quarter of that cover and normalize the remainder to get a doubly stochastic matrix on the same support. Thus the all-zero imbalance vector is a convex combination using any specified imbalance vector. A linear functional on the imbalances that is nonzero on some cover must take both positive and negative values. If the functional vanishes on all covers, it makes no distinction between them.

## Arbitrary linear edge costs can also tie an unpaired cover

Take $(x\lor y\lor z)\land(x\lor y\lor w)$. The three assignments $xyzw=1111,0011,1000$ have unique paired covers whose union uses every supported edge. The first uses all wires and both auxiliary loops in each clause. The second leaves the first two ports of each clause available, forcing the first port to $A$ and the middle port to $B$. The third leaves the last two ports available, forcing the middle port to $A$ and the last port to $B$. The variable blocks in these three covers use both of their possible local states.

There is an unpaired cover. Run a directed cycle from the $x$ block into the first clause, go through $A$ to the $y$ block, and follow the $y$ block's internal edge into its second occurrence. Run through $A$ of the second clause back to the $x$ block and close the cycle with its internal edge. The untouched third ports can each form a two-cycle with their clause's $B$, while the $z$ and $w$ blocks use their single-index loops.

Let $S$ be the multiset sum of the three paired covers. Subtract that unpaired cover from $S$, which contains every supported edge. The residual bipartite multigraph is two-regular and decomposes into two perfect matchings. Thus the three paired covers have exactly the same aggregate edge multiplicities as three covers including the unpaired one. Under any additive edge costs, if the three paired covers are all minimum-cost, then all three covers in the second decomposition have that same minimum cost. This rules out an edge-cost filter on this compiler that preserves an optimum cover for every satisfying assignment while excluding all unpaired covers. The argument leaves open decision reductions that preserve only some satisfying assignments or change the representation.
